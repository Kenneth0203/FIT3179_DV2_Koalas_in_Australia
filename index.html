01 / Understanding the numbers

How many koalas are estimated in each region?

The monitoring programme reports separate estimates for Queensland, New South Wales and the ACT, and for Victoria and South Australia. Each line shows the lowest and highest published estimate.

Estimated number of koalas by region in 2025

Hover over a line for exact values. The national estimate is calculated separately, so do not add the regional ranges together. Source: National Koala Monitoring Program.

A larger estimate does not necessarily mean the population grew. Survey coverage and modelling improved between releases, so estimates from different years cannot be directly compared as a trend.

03 / Queensland hospital records

What do Queensland’s koala records show?

KoalaBase contains 59,808 reports from July 1996 to June 2025. The reports include sightings, health problems and contact with hospitals. They do not count every koala in Queensland, and changes over time may partly reflect how reporting changed.

Number of KoalaBase reports each year

The chart shows complete years from 1997 to 2024. It counts reports received, not the number of koalas living in Queensland.

Why koalas were reported or admitted

The bars count reasons or conditions recorded in KoalaBase. One report can contain several, so the bars overlap and should not be added together.

What happened after a koala was reported?

The five main outcomes are shown. Three rare outcomes—found dead, unable to locate and no outcome recorded—total 147 reports, less than 0.3%, and are omitted so the chart is easier to read.

Common recorded health problems by year

Conjunctivitis means inflammation around the eyes, which can cause redness or discharge. Cystitis means inflammation of the bladder or urinary tract. Wasted condition means the koala was severely underweight or in very poor body condition. One report can include more than one condition.

Common recorded threats and accidents by year

Each line counts reports tagged with that event. “Dog attack or interaction” means suspected or confirmed dog contact; it does not always prove that a dog caused the injury. One report can have more than one tag, so do not add the lines together.

Reports that include a release date

This is the percentage of reports that contain a release date. It is not a survival rate or treatment success rate.

04 / Locations and movement

Where were koalas reported in Queensland?

Most hospital reports in this dataset came from southeast Queensland’s coast. These maps show the areas with the most reports and common movements between rescue and release.

Hospital reports by council area

Larger circles mean more reports. Each circle is placed at the average recorded location within that council area.

Areas with the most hospital reports

Darker squares mean more reports in that area. This shows where reports were concentrated, not where all koalas live. Hover to see coordinates to two decimal places.

Most common movements from rescue to release

Only the 15 most common routes are shown. Orange lines connect rescue and release areas; thicker lines mean more reports. Dark dots mark release areas. Hover to see coordinates to two decimal places.
